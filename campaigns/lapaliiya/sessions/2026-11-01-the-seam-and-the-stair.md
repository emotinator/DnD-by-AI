## Journal — the party's chronicle

**Day one on the mountain.** We woke to an empty post. Bubba is gone; no word, no farewell, only prints that the wind took. Wolves circled us all night and never came in. At dawn a fairy named Paz landed on the warm stones and looked at Kaitlin as if she'd been dreamed. Paz's sacred pool, kept for a thousand years in Eldath's name, went dry overnight. The trail led here. To her.

Then Valerian came over the ridge, half-starved, a bark-bound book at his hip that burned its runes into him. He'd been underground for weeks. He followed a line of gold light in the rock to find us.

We argued. Everyone had a god and a reason. Kaitlin told us to stop, and Arinir asked her where *she* wanted to go. She pointed east, toward the steam.

We gave the wolves back their den, left them food, rubbed their fur into our cloaks, and walked the bare rock. A great white wolf watched us leave and lay down.

At midday we found Paz's water, or where it went: an old channel, reopened, that turned against the slope and poured into a crack along the seam. Kaitlin touched it. "It's thirsty," she said. "Five fingers hold it. There used to be more." Arinir gave her his lantern, and in its light we all saw the chain: links like cartwheels, one cracked, and a thread of gold running from it to her.

At the steam vent we found a boiling pool with Paz's lilies in it, a buried ring of carved stones, and a stranger's chalk: a seven-pointed star, two points struck out, and the word *Faster*. A raven told Valerian the man comes after every big shake. He went down today and hadn't come back.

We fought four steam spirits on the broken stair and pulled him out. Teodar Vessin, a surveyor paid by Amn. He knew Kaitlin's face from a sketch in the Factor's study. The Factor wants her found. Amn is digging under the city into something old. And a woman in a hood pays Teodar to count the chains as they break.

We camped at the rim in the snow. Hurintha left at first light for Davarn, to warn the dwarves and bring back Dave. She gave Kaitlin a stone and told her not to let anyone decide for her.

Tomorrow: down to the chain, or into the city after the woman who's counting.

## Novella — "The Seam and the Stair"

### I. The Empty Post

The hollow was not as they had left it.

Grey light found the vent first, then the frost on the bedrolls, then the place by the north gap where Bubba Fatt should have been. His bow was gone. So was he. A line of soft prints led out between the columns and vanished where the wind had scoured the ridge to bare black stone.

Other tracks stayed. Wolves, a dozen or more, circling the ring of basalt in a patient loop, and among them two sets as wide as a spread hand. Whatever had made them had paced the hollow all night and never come in.

On the warm stone by the north gap sat a stranger the size of a sparrow, on wings like frosted glass, studying the sleepers' faces as if matching them to a dream.

"Oh," said Paz. "It's *you*."

She meant Kaitlin.

Paz of the Lucht Síochána bowed, far too formally for something so small. Her people kept still waters in Eldath's name; the Pool of Lucht Síochána had held cold, quiet water for a thousand years, and last night it had simply gone. "The paths of the world have a grain," Paz said, "like wood. Last night it broke, and the break ran downhill. I followed it. It ended here." One tiny finger rose. "At you."

And over the ridge, gaunt and bark-scorched, came Valerian. Three weeks in the dark, or three cycles; down there time was measured by the hum of the rock. The Codex at his hip had bitten its runes into his side and pointed him upward, and on the surface a thread of gold in the stone had led him west to faces he knew.

### II. Every God and Every Reason

It started reasonably and got worse.

Paz wanted the pool. Nelgron would not hand Kaitlin to a stranger's goddess on a feeling. Melania observed that tiny winged strangers claiming destiny at dawn were exactly what Loki would send. Hurintha wanted something she could put an axe through. Arinir wanted to know who had food. Valerian unrolled a map no one looked at. Fire and water, mercy and peace and mischief: every reason was a good one.

Then Kaitlin stood.

"Stop."

The stone heard it. The hollow shivered, the vent coughed steam, and pale gold ran through the basalt and faded. A howl came down off the ridge.

"Every one of you is talking about where to take me. Nobody's asked me where I'm going."

Arinir, who had once shouted at her to run, asked her now. "You get to choose. As you did before."

She crouched by Valerian's forgotten map and traced the ridge east to the smudge marked *steam*. "It's pulling me there. Like a hand on my sleeve. I'm not going to be anyone's keeper. I want to see it. I want to know what it wants with me before it decides for me." A beat. "I'd rather not go alone."

### III. The Wolves' Den

Melania read the tracks as if they were scripture. The small prints were shallow, uneven: starving. The great ones had walked the same loop again and again, always returning to a worn hollow behind the eastern columns, scattered with old fur.

"This is their den," she said. "They're not hunting us. They're waiting for us to leave their home."

So they left it as they'd found it. Arinir laid a neat line of rations on a flat stone at the north gap, the way you'd set a table for a guest. He worked the pack's own shed fur into his cloak and boots, and the others did the same, until they smelled of wolf and ash and old smoke. Then he led them onto the bare black spine of the ridge, wind in their faces, leaving no prints.

Half a mile on, Melania looked back. A lean grey wolf nosed the rations. Above it, on a column, a huge pale shape sat watching them go, frost curling from its jaws.

Then it lay down. Nothing followed.

### IV. The Stolen Water

At midday a dry gully crossed their path, smooth-worn, fringed with plants gone black and curled as if burned. Paz dropped into it and said nothing for a long moment.

"This is not where our water should be. Eldath's water doesn't *flow*. It rises, it rests, it reflects. That is the entire point of it." Arinir, under his breath, wondered at a pool that ran downhill, and received the full fury of something four inches tall.

The damp trail did not run down toward the valley. It turned, against the grain of the land, and ran straight across the slope to the seam, where the rock was split in a fresh, wet crack breathing warm air. Deep below, something hissed like water on a forge.

Kaitlin laid her palm on the stone. The seam brightened like a coal under breath.

"It's not angry," she said. "It's thirsty. It's waking slowly, like someone who's slept so long they don't remember going to sleep. It's held. Like fingers around a wrist." A pause. "Five fingers. There used to be more."

Melania paced and theorised: seven chains, seven lantern-bearers, seven hands that held the world together *before there were gods*. What if the seven were gods once, and spent themselves building this, and that is why no one can name them?

"It doesn't feel like a god," Kaitlin said. "It feels older than praying."

Paz asked the question none of them wanted. "If it wakes, will it put this world at risk?"

"Probably," Kaitlin said. "Something held down that long will knock things over when it gets up, even if it doesn't mean to. But they chained it because they were afraid, and then everyone forgot why." She looked up. "Would you be kind, if you woke up in chains?"

Then Arinir held out his lantern, lit, and Kaitlin took it.

The flame went gold. In its light the stone turned faint as dark water, and below lay a chain, each link the size of a cartwheel, half light and half memory, running east into the dark. The link beneath the crack was cracked, and water steamed where it seeped through. And rising from the chain, thin as spider silk, a single gold thread reached up out of the earth and stopped at Kaitlin, slack, like the end of a rope someone had offered her once and she had never taken.

"Do you see that?" she whispered.

They all saw it.

### V. The Counting Stones

They followed the seam east as the afternoon wore down. The sky was the hard washed blue of high winter; Karrag-Dum shrank into the haze behind them, all black spires and red forge-glow; and the snow thinned to grey ash that steamed in the sun. Dead pines leaned away from the east as if pushed. Every trickle of meltwater ran toward the column.

From the last rise they saw it: a roaring white pillar tearing upward out of a bowl in the mountain, and at its foot a boiling milky pool where none had been a week ago. Pale lilies, boiled white, turned on its surface. Paz made a small sound. Around the pool, half-buried in the hillside, stood a ring of black columns cut far too true to be natural. And in the ash, one set of hard-soled city boots led down to the water.

Melania's read was certain: whoever this was had not been surprised. They knelt to measure, not to pray. They walked into the ring the way you walk into a room you've been in before.

On the columns, Paz found the chalk: tallies, one per visit, each with a number in a neat hand, rising. Today's nearly doubled. At the top of the largest column, a seven-pointed star with two points struck through, and beneath it, crisp and fresh, one word.

*Faster.*

A raven in a dead pine traded gossip for dried meat. The man with the hard feet and the box came from the big hot nest, it said, the day after every big shake. He put shiny sticks in the water and scratched the stones with white and went down the dark mouth. Today he went down and never came up.

Inside the ring, a stair dropped into steam. A rope was tied to a column, running down. Arinir laid two fingers on it like a pulse. Dead weight. A weak twitch. And between, a thrum: five beats, then a pause.

### VI. The Stair

Valerian sent a bat down. Through its echoing senses he found the man two-thirds of the way down, pinned under a slab where the stair had sheared away, muttering *they have to know*. Below him the stair opened into a hall: columns, a carved channel built to bring water in on purpose, and at its heart a basalt pillar wrapped in a chain of gold, solid here, one link cracked and hissing where the water poured over it. A tall figure was carved on the pillar with its arms spread and its face worn to nothing. Beside it, a niche the size of a lantern. Empty.

Then the steam spirits noticed.

Melania's Guiding Bolt lit the stair white and burned the first mephit half through. Paz, sulking and silver-voiced, wrapped three of them in Aid. The mephits came up hissing; one breathed scalding steam across the lip and scalded Melania, and the steam washed off Nelgron's infernal blood. Nelgron struck the first to land and, when the deck turned in the heroes' favour, moved twice in the space of one: two mephits dead, each shoved off the lip before it burst so the steam bloomed in open air. Valerian's ice knives punched the third apart. Arinir crept onto the stub of the broken stair and put two arrows into the last, and it burst beside the trapped man, who curled away from it and lived.

Paz flew down, looked the man in his red-lined coat in the eye, and said coldly, "You are standing in my water." Then Paz shrank the slab to the size of a loaf, and the rest of them heaved.

Nobody had thought about the angle. He swung out over the gap and slammed into the far wall; Arinir caught him at the cost of his ribs; the ledge gave way behind him and took the man's box and instruments rattling into the boiling channel below. But they had him, and he still had the satchel clutched to his chest.

### VII. Teodar

"Who are you? You're not from the embassy."

Arinir asked him whether he knew her face, whether his people had put her in chains. Teodar Vessin went grey. There was a sketch in the Factor's study, he said, beside the shipping manifests. *The Hawklin girl.* Except it wasn't. They called her a loose end. The Factor still had people asking after her in the lower markets.

Paz demanded the water back. He hadn't taken it, he swore; he only measured it, and for weeks the numbers had climbed, and the Factor only said *keep measuring* and kept digging. Amn's diggers had broken into something under Karrag-Dum last month: a vault of black stone, walls too straight. Since then the mountain shook.

Valerian asked about the hall. Teodar had been down four times; this vent was only days old. The gold light wasn't fire. The chain's crack had been a hairline on his first visit and could take fingers now. Deeper in there was a frieze: seven figures, all different shapes, every face chiselled smooth by hand, long ago. He gave them his charcoal rubbing. And the niche: clean, he said, as if whatever had been in it was lifted out with care.

And the star? Someone at the embassy, not the Factor, had asked him to draw it at every site, and to strike a point whenever the glow suddenly dimmed. Twice now. The second time, about a week ago.

"A woman," he said. "She wears a hood indoors, always. She pays better than the Factor. And she frightens me more."

### VIII. The Fire at the Rim

They did not go down. Snow came out of a slate sky and melted before it touched the warm ash of the rim. They camped in the lee of a tilted column. Teodar slept with his arms locked around his satchel. Paz sat alone by the boiling pool, the way one sits beside a grave.

When the fire was low, Hurintha spoke. "The dwarves dig. If this chain runs through the earth, it runs under them too. Nobody in Davarn knows why the ground shakes." She stood, enormous in the ember-light. "You lot are good at thinking. I'm good at walking. I'll warn the dwarves, tell the blind priest, and get Dave. He doesn't like being on his own." And Theon and the singer, if she could find them.

She went around the fire: a hand on a shoulder, a nod, a gripped forearm. At Kaitlin she stopped and pressed a small grey stone into her palm, worn smooth by years in a pocket. "My people's. The old ones watch whoever holds it. Don't let anyone decide for you."

By first light her prints led west through fresh snow, past the ring of wolf tracks. The wolf tracks did not follow.

Long after, two were still awake. Valerian watched the Codex draw the seam onto a new page, east and down. Kaitlin sat with the stone in one hand and the lantern in the other.

"You keep looking at me like I'm a compass."

"You're pointing somewhere," he said. "So is this. They point the same way."

She laid it out, as plainly as a map. Something old chained under the mountain, seven chains, two broken, pulling at her. Amn digging and making it worse. The Factor hunting her. A woman in a hood counting the chains as they break.

"Somebody wanted the seven forgotten," Valerian said, and looked at the rubbing. "With a chisel. Things don't get forgotten by accident that carefully."

"So we go down and see the chain," Kaitlin said. "Or we go into the city and find the woman who's counting."

"Which would you pick?"

"I'd want to know who's counting," he said, "before I went near the thing being counted."

She almost smiled. "I'd want to see it before anyone else decides what it is."

The snow kept falling. Beneath them the mountain breathed, five beats and a rest, a little faster than it had that morning.
